# Benchmark provenance

On an established channel an average is a rough average of a lot of accounts. On
a new one it is often a handful of accounts, one agency's book of clients, or a
sentence that has been repeated until it looks like a fact. **A number that
appears in ten articles may have one origin.**

## Ask five questions of any quoted number

1. **Who measured it?** A named dataset, a platform statement, an advertiser's
   own account, or nobody traceable. "Industry average" with no owner is the
   last of these.
2. **How much spend and how many accounts sit behind it?** One advertiser with a
   modest budget is an anecdote. Say so.
3. **Which window?** A launch-month figure on a surface that has changed since
   is out of date.
4. **Which definition?** Click-through on all clicks or only outbound ones.
   Conversion on a platform pixel or on the backend. Cost including or excluding
   fees. Two figures with the same name often count different things.
5. **Who benefits from you believing it?** A vendor selling a fix, an agency
   selling management, a platform selling inventory. This does not make the
   number false; it makes it a claim to test, not evidence.

## Count the origins, not the citations

Follow each source one step upstream. If several trace to the same single
study, post or press release, you have one data point cited many times. Write
"3 independent origins" or "1 origin, 9 citations" next to the number.

## Grade it

**Usable as a range.** A named source, a stated window and definition, and
meaningful spend. Use the spread, not the midpoint.

**Directional only.** Traceable but small, old, or defined differently from
yours. Good for "which order of magnitude", not for a target.

**Do not use.** No traceable origin, or the only origin is someone selling
against it. Say that in writing, so the number does not return in the next
deck.

## When sources disagree

Disagreement is information. If two credible sources give figures that differ
by a large factor, do not average them. Find what differs: the definition, the
window, the advertiser mix, the objective. Often the gap is explained by one of
these and then neither number was wrong.

Never present a range as a target. A benchmark tells you what others report. It
does not tell you what you can achieve or what you can afford.

## Output

Each quoted figure with its origin, the independent-origin count, the window,
the definition, the grade, and what it can and cannot be used for. A single line
for anything graded do-not-use saying why.

## What this does not cover

Deciding what to do with no usable benchmark, see `no-benchmark-verdict`.
Checking the platform's own reported numbers against your records, which is a
reconciliation job rather than a provenance one.

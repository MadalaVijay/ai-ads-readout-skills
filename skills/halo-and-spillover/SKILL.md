# Looking for effects outside the report

Some surfaces report clicks and conversions poorly or not at all, and some work
by raising awareness rather than driving an immediate visit. People then claim a
"halo": the surface helps, just not where the report looks. **That is possible
and it is also the most convenient excuse in advertising.** This skill is how to
test it without fooling yourself.

## Say what the halo would look like, before looking

Name the specific signals that would move if the surface were helping, and
roughly when. Candidates:

- **Brand-name search volume,** from people who saw you and then looked you up.
- **Direct and typed visits.**
- **Self-reported source,** a "how did you hear about us" field, ideally with the
  new surface as an explicit option.
- **Conversion rate on your other channels,** if familiarity makes them convert
  better.

Writing these down first stops you choosing, after the fact, whichever signal
happens to have moved.

## Check the timing

An effect has to follow the cause. Mark the day spend started and the day it
changed. Then look for the signal moving **after** the start, in step with the
spend level, and settling when spend stops. A lift that began before the
campaign, or that continues unchanged after you switch it off, is not the
campaign.

## Rule out the usual alternatives

Brand search and direct traffic move for many reasons. Before crediting the
surface, list what else changed in the same window: other campaigns, a launch,
seasonality, a press mention, a promotion, a change in tracking. If several
things moved at once you cannot separate them. Say that, do not pick one.

## Treat self-reported source with care

It is cheap and genuinely useful, and also biased: people remember the last
thing they saw, favour channels they find memorable, and skip the field. Read it
as a direction and track the share over time. Do not read it as a precise
attribution, and do not compare a self-reported share with a click-based share
as if they measured the same thing.

## How much to believe

A halo claim built on one coincident signal and no ruling-out is a story. One
built on several signals moving in step with spend, a clear start and end, and
the alternatives checked is a reasonable hypothesis. **Neither is proof that the
surface caused the sales.** Only a holdout test, where a comparable group does
not see the ads, can show that, and it needs enough volume to be readable.

## Do not let halo rescue a failing test

If the direct outcome cost is far outside your ceiling, a vague halo is rarely
enough to justify continuing. Make the halo case before the spend, with the
signals named, or treat it as a reason to run a proper holdout, not as a reason
to keep paying.

## Output

The signals named in advance, the start date and spend profile, the movement in
each signal with its timing, the other changes in the window, the confidence
level in plain words, and whether a holdout test is warranted.

## What this does not cover

Designing and running the holdout itself, and the statistics of reading it. This
skill decides whether a halo claim is worth that effort.
